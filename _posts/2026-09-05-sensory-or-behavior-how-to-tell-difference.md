As a mom of two and a pediatric OT, I have experienced toddler energy both personally and professionally. I often find myself looking for a deeper meaning behind a tantrum or meltdown. Sometimes there is one. Other times, the answer really is, *“She’s just 2.”*

So how do you know if there is something bigger going on?

The answer is not always clear. **[Sensory dysregulation]({{ '/topics/sensory-processing/' | relative_url }})** can result in what looks like “behavior,” but that behavior is often a child’s way of communicating that their nervous system is overwhelmed or that they are in distress.

When I am trying to determine what is really going on with my own children, I walk myself through the questions below. Admittedly, it is much easier to do this with my clients because I am not as emotionally close to the situation. But before assuming a behavior is simply “bad behavior,” ask yourself these questions:

---

## 1. Was there an obvious antecedent?

What happened immediately before the behavior?

Was your child hurt, scared, frustrated, or told no? Did someone take their toy? Did they have to stop doing something they enjoyed?

These details matter, but I also like to think of sensory overwhelm as a 2-liter bottle of soda. Every small challenge shakes the bottle: someone takes their toy, they fall down, they are asked to try something new. Eventually, something small may cause the bottle to open and explode.

When the reaction seems much bigger than the situation itself, consider whether your child was already overwhelmed.

**Try:**
* **Comforting your child.**
* **Moving to a new environment.** I love bringing my kids outside.
* **Offering a simple distraction,** such as reading a book.
* **Validating their feelings:** *“Wow, that was a little scary.”* Validation helps children understand their feelings and learn how to handle similar challenges in the future.

---

## 2. What was happening in the environment?

Consider what was going on around your child.

Was it a quiet day at home? Were you in a new place? Was it loud, crowded, unpredictable, very hot, or very cold?

All of these factors can add up.

Most children can adjust to a new environment after a few experiences. However, if you consistently notice irritability or overwhelm in loud or unpredictable places, such as indoor play areas, there may be a sensory component worth exploring.

---

## 3. How long does the behavior last?

Kids are going to have big feelings. Tantrums are part of how they learn to manage frustration and communicate their needs.

However, most tantrums eventually resolve.

If you have already tried co-regulating, comforting your child, changing the environment, and addressing basic needs like hunger and fatigue, but your child continues to struggle for an extended period of time, consider whether sensory overwhelm may be contributing.

---

## 4. Does giving in immediately stop the behavior?

This is not a perfect test, but it can provide some helpful information.

* **If your child was told no,** you change your mind and give them what they wanted, and they immediately stop, the behavior may be related to wanting access to something.
* **On the other hand,** if you have bent over backwards to make things better and your child continues to struggle even after getting what they wanted, you may be looking at a meltdown rather than a typical tantrum.

---

## 5. Is the behavior connected to a specific situation?

Look for patterns.

Does it happen during transitions? At bedtime? At mealtimes? When entering a particular environment?

Children with sensory processing difficulties may become comfortable in one environment and learn how to manage the sensations around them. When they transition to a different environment, they have to process a whole new set of sensory information.

Transitions can also be difficult when a child has previously experienced sensory overwhelm in that location. Their past experiences can influence how they respond the next time they enter that environment.

If meltdowns consistently happen at a certain time of day, first consider basic needs such as hunger and fatigue. Then look for other factors. For example, if mealtimes are consistently difficult, consider whether the sensory experience of eating may be contributing.

---

## What Can You Do?

Whether you are dealing with a tantrum or sensory meltdown, your first job is to help your child feel safe and supported.

### If it is primarily behavior:

* **Validate their feelings:** *“You are really frustrated that we have to leave.”*
* **Maintain your boundary:** This is much easier said than done. Giving in is often the fastest way to stop a tantrum in the moment, but consistency matters.
* **Change the environment:** Move to another room or, ideally, go outside if you can. A simple change of environment can provide a reset.
* **Skip the teaching moment:** When your child is highly upset, they are not ready to learn a lesson. Wait until they are calm before talking about what happened.
* **Use consistent language:** I like using the terms “expected” and “unexpected” behavior. Praise expected behavior when you see it: *“You came when Mommy called your name. That was awesome and expected!”* You can also use the language in everyday situations: *“Oh, I dropped something. That was unexpected!”* This helps children learn that unexpected things happen and that we can respond to them.
* **Check yourself:** I love and hate this one. I know that when I am stressed, rushing, or dysregulated, my kids absolutely pick up on it, which can lead to more dysregulation. Try saying your feelings out loud: *“I am really stressed because we are going to be late. I am going to take some deep breaths and stay calm.”* You are modeling the exact skill you want your child to learn.
* **Help your child recognize their body signals:** This is called **interoception**, or our ability to notice signals from inside our bodies. Start simple: *“Your hands feel sticky.” “You look sleepy.” “You just yawned.”* Then connect those signals to their needs: *“It is almost lunchtime. Check in with your tummy. Are you hungry?”* Staying ahead of hunger, fatigue, and other basic needs can make a big difference.
* **Use tools that help children understand their regulation:** Programs such as *The Alert Program (The Engine Program)* and *Zones of Regulation* can give children language to recognize their emotions and arousal levels and identify when they are becoming dysregulated.

And remember: sometimes it really is just a phase. As long as we are doing our part as adults—managing our own behavior, validating their feelings, and maintaining appropriate boundaries—our children will continue to develop these skills.

---

### If it is sensory:

Many of the same strategies still apply, but reducing sensory input becomes especially important.

* **Move to a less stimulating environment:** Reduce noise, talking, and visual stimulation. If you are in the car, try quiet time instead of adding more conversation or stimulation.
* **Use targeted [heavy work]({{ '/topics/sensory-processing/' | relative_url }}):** Instead of simply telling your child to “go run around,” give them a specific, purposeful activity:
  * Push a laundry basket across the floor.
  * Bear crawl to you and give you a high five.
  * Crab walk back.
  * Help carry groceries.
  * Help with gardening.
  * Wipe down tables or windows.
  
  Kids often love helping because it gives them a sense of purpose while also providing meaningful sensory input.
* **Create a safe space:** A tent, teepee, or cozy corner that provides some separation from the environment can give an overwhelmed child a place to reset. You can add simple calming tools such as a chewy snack, a visual toy, or something safe to squeeze.

---

## Reach Out to an OT When You Need Help

Sensory regulation is not one-size-fits-all. There is no single sensory strategy that works for every child, and blanket recommendations are not always appropriate.

A pediatric OT can look at your child's individual sensory needs and help you determine what types of input may be helpful, when to use them, and how to build them into your child's daily routines.

If your child's behavior consistently seems connected to sensory overwhelm and it is affecting your family's day-to-day life, please [reach out for support](/contact/).

Most importantly, remember that our job as parents is not to prevent every tantrum or meltdown. Our job is to help our children understand what their bodies and emotions are telling them, provide support when they are overwhelmed, and teach them the skills they need to manage those big feelings over time.
